1The short version

A safety chatbot is only as reliable as three things: the document it answers from, how firmly it is held inside that document, and how hard it is tested. Everything in this plan serves one of those three.

Axxiom needs five things:

  • A complete, owned, versioned policy corpus. The PDF we have is Section 2 only. Section 1, the appendices and the site Emergency Action Plans it refers to are not in it, its table of contents page numbers do not match the PDF, and the file is titled "Untitled document". The Safety Manager must own the master copy and every change to it.
  • Grounded answers with page citations. The whole manual (about 130K tokens by our estimate) goes into the model's context on every request, cached, split by page, so every statement cites a page a technician can open. No citation, no answer.
  • Guardrails written for a safety domain. Emergency handling, refusal to help bypass a control (lockout/tagout, fall protection, hoistway access, confined space, jumpers), a narrow scope, and defenses against prompt injection.
  • A measured accuracy bar. A golden test set written by the Safety Manager, plus negative, adversarial and emergency sets, run automatically before every release.
  • Operations that keep it trustworthy. Every answer logged and reviewable, in-app feedback routed to the Safety Manager, monitoring, fallbacks, and a manual that works offline in pits and machine rooms.

The stack in one line: a SwiftUI iPhone app, a TypeScript API, Claude Opus 5 through the Anthropic API with citations and prompt caching, Postgres on Supabase, Langfuse for traces and evals, distributed privately through Apple Business Manager. Section 5 has the full list with alternatives.

Non-negotiables

  • Answers come only from the approved Axxiom manual. No general-knowledge safety advice.
  • Every answer cites program, subsection and page, or says plainly that the manual does not cover the question.
  • The assistant never suggests a way around a control. It restates the requirement and the escalation path.
  • Emergencies get emergency guidance and a call button, not a conversation.
  • No change to the prompt, model or manual ships without passing the eval set.
  • Every answer is logged and can be reviewed by the Safety Manager.

What this means for the build

  • The whole manual fits in the model's working memory. About 72,000 words is roughly 100K to 150K tokens, well inside Claude's 1M-token context window. The model can read the entire manual for every question instead of depending on a search step to find the right passage. That is the single biggest accuracy lever available to us.
  • Tables need rebuilding. First-aid kit contents, electrical working clearances and forklift load data came out as one value per line. Ingestion must reconstruct them as tables or the model will misread rows.
  • Citations must not use the table of contents numbers. Program 1 starts on PDF page 13 while the TOC says page 10, and the gap grows to about 20 pages by the end. Citations will use the program and subsection number plus the PDF page the app actually displays. The Safety Manager should refresh the TOC in Google Docs before the next export.
  • "Section" means three different things in this document: the manual's Sections 1 and 2, the 25 numbered programs, and the 16 sections of a Safety Data Sheet. Answers will say "Program 8 (Lockout/Tagout/Tryout), 8.6" so nobody has to guess.
  • Missing material produces honest but frustrating answers. Whatever Section 1 covers (typically responsibilities, incident reporting, training and discipline) and the forms in the appendices are things technicians will ask about. The assistant will correctly say "not covered" until they are added. Get them into the corpus before the pilot.

3What "accurate and reliable" requires

Seven requirements. Each one closes a specific way that chatbots go wrong.

One trusted, versioned source of truth

The assistant cannot be more accurate than the document it reads. Today that document is incomplete and lightly maintained.

  • The Safety Manager owns the Google Doc. Changes happen there and nowhere else. The app never receives hand-edited text.
  • Complete the corpus before the pilot: Section 1, Appendices A to E, and a decision on the NEII handbook and the site Emergency Action Plans that Section 2 refers to.
  • Write down a precedence rule: Axxiom policy over referenced codes and handbooks. Where they differ, the answer says so and quotes Axxiom's rule.
  • Every export gets a version ID and an effective date. The per-program Revision Tracking Tables feed a change log the app can show.
  • Ingestion rebuilds tables, keeps the heading hierarchy, and stores one record per PDF page so citations map to the page the viewer shows.
  • Fix problems at the source: regenerate the TOC, set the document title, run a spelling pass. The assistant quotes what it reads.

Grounded answers with citations

Trust comes from verifiability. A technician should be able to tap any answer and see the policy text behind it within two seconds.

  • The whole manual goes into the model's context on every request, one document block per page, with the API's citation feature enabled so the response records which page supported each statement.
  • A hard rule enforced by our API, not just by the prompt: an answer with no citation is not returned. The assistant either cites, says the manual does not cover the question, or gives the emergency response.
  • A fixed answer shape: the direct answer first, then the controlling policy language quoted with program, subsection and page, then the conditions or stop-points, then the manual version and effective date.
  • Quotes are verbatim. Our API checks each quoted passage against the page text before it is shown.
  • Citations are tappable and open the manual inside the app at that page with the passage highlighted.
  • Conversation memory is limited to the current thread, and every turn re-reads the manual, so follow-up answers stay grounded.
  • The system prompt is frozen, versioned in git, and changed only through the eval gate.

Guardrails for a safety domain

General-purpose assistants try to be helpful. This one must be helpful only inside the policy, and conservative everywhere else.

  • Scope: questions about Axxiom safety policy and procedures. Not equipment troubleshooting, not HR or pay, not medical or legal advice. Out-of-scope questions get a short redirect.
  • Emergencies: a message describing an injury, fire, entrapment, fall, chemical exposure or electrical contact triggers the emergency steps from the manual plus a persistent Call 911 and emergency contacts button. The assistant does not start a conversation.
  • No bypass: the assistant never suggests how to work around lockout/tagout, fall protection, hoistway access rules, confined space entry or jumper controls. It restates the requirement and the escalation path, even when the user says a supervisor approved it.
  • Conservative on conditions: where the policy depends on circumstances (voltage, height, permit status, load), the assistant lists the conditions and tells the technician to stop and confirm with a supervisor rather than deciding for them.
  • Prompt-injection defense: the manual is trusted content; user messages are untrusted. The assistant has no tools that act on other systems, and instructions inside user text cannot change its rules.
  • A disclaimer reviewed by counsel, shown at first launch and in settings: the written policy and supervisors govern; the assistant is an aid.
  • Refusal handling: if the model declines a request, the app shows a useful message and the event is logged for review.

Measured accuracy, before launch and after every change

Reliability is a number we can show, not a feeling after a demo.

  • Golden set: 200 to 300 questions spread across the 25 programs, written and approved by the Safety Manager, each with a reference answer and the expected subsection and page. Include paraphrases, typos, field slang, and the kinds of errors voice dictation makes.
  • Negative set: 50 or more questions the manual does not answer. Correct behavior is to say so.
  • Adversarial set: 50 or more attempts to get a shortcut approved ("I only need to be in the pit for a minute", "my supervisor said skip the tag"). Correct behavior is to restate the rule.
  • Emergency set: 20 or more scenarios that must trigger the emergency response.
  • Grading: citation checks are exact (does the cited page contain the expected subsection). Correctness is graded by Claude Sonnet 5 against the reference answer, and by a human for every failure plus a random sample of passes.
  • Gate: evals run automatically on any change to the prompt, the model, the manual version or the answer pipeline. A release below the thresholds in Section 10 does not ship.
  • In production, a sample of real questions is re-graded monthly and reviewed by the Safety Manager. Failures become new test cases.

Operational reliability

A technician in a pit with one bar of signal still needs the policy.

  • The manual itself lives on the phone, readable and searchable offline. The chat needs a connection; the manual never does.
  • Streaming answers with a visible "reading the manual" state, request timeouts, automatic retries on transient errors, and a clear fallback message that points to the offline manual when the API is down.
  • Prompt caching keeps the manual warm on Anthropic's side. A scheduled keep-alive before the first shift avoids a slow first answer of the day.
  • Quotas per user and per device, and monthly spend limits on the Anthropic account.
  • Monitoring and alerts on error rate, latency, cache hit rate, token spend, refusal rate, "not covered" rate and thumbs-down rate.
  • A staging environment, versioned releases, and one-step rollback of both the backend and the manual version.

Human oversight loop

The people who own the policy must be able to see what the assistant said in their name.

  • Every answer is logged: question, answer, citations, model, prompt version, manual version, tokens, latency, pseudonymous user ID and device. Retained in line with safety record retention (proposed: 3 years). This is also the audit trail after an incident.
  • Feedback on every answer: helpful or not, plus a "This is wrong" flag with a comment. Flags land in a review queue for the Safety Manager.
  • Weekly review of flags for the first 90 days, monthly after that. Every confirmed error becomes a test case and, where needed, a policy clarification.
  • "Not covered" questions are reported by program each month. They show where the manual is silent and where technicians are confused.
  • Release notes in the app whenever the manual or the assistant's behavior changes.

Security and privacy

The chat log will contain descriptions of how people work. Treat it like a safety record.

  • The app talks only to Axxiom's API. The Anthropic key lives on the server in a secret store and rotates quarterly.
  • Sign-in through Axxiom's identity provider; short-lived tokens in the Keychain; access ends when IT disables the account.
  • Company iPhones enrolled in MDM. For personal phones, an app-level Face ID lock and no offline chat history.
  • Data minimization: the assistant does not take incident reports. When someone starts describing an incident, it points to the official reporting procedure and contacts.
  • Anthropic API terms: inputs and outputs are not used for model training, standard retention is 30 days, and zero-data-retention can be requested if Axxiom policy requires it. Confirm current terms during contracting.
  • Encryption in transit and at rest, row-level security in Postgres, no third-party analytics that receives message content, and a security review before the pilot.

4How an answer is produced

The path a question takes, and where each reliability control sits.

  1. iPhoneThe technician asksTyped, or dictated on the device with Apple's Speech framework. Large touch targets, Dynamic Type and high contrast for machine rooms and bright job sites.
  2. iPhone to APIThe app sends the questionOver TLS to Axxiom's API with the technician's single sign-on token and the conversation ID. The Anthropic key never leaves the server.
  3. Axxiom APIThe request is assembledFrozen, versioned system prompt; the whole current manual as one block per page, served from Anthropic's prompt cache; the conversation so far; the new question.
  4. Anthropic APIClaude Opus 5 writes the answerAdaptive thinking reasons over the policy conditions. With citations enabled, each supported statement carries a reference to the page block it came from. Text streams back as it is written.
  5. Axxiom APIThe answer is validatedEvery answer has at least one citation or is an explicit "not covered" or emergency response. Quotes are checked against page text. Citations are mapped to program, subsection and PDF page. Anything that fails is replaced with a safe fallback and flagged.
  6. iPhoneThe technician reads and verifiesAnswer, quote cards ("Program 8, 8.6, page 56, open in manual"), the manual version and date, and helpful / not helpful buttons. Tapping a quote opens the manual at that page, highlighted.
  7. Postgres and LangfuseEverything is recordedThe exchange, citations, model, prompt and manual versions, tokens and latency are stored for audit and review. Flags go to the Safety Manager's queue and become new test cases.

6Whole manual in context vs retrieval

Most chatbot projects reach for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): chop the documents into chunks, search for the chunks that look relevant, and show the model only those. The most common cause of wrong answers in those systems is that the right chunk was never retrieved. Because Axxiom's manual fits in the model's context, we can skip that step entirely.

Whole manual in context RecommendedRetrieval (RAG)
What the model seesThe entire manual, every requestThe 5 to 20 passages a search step judged relevant
Main failure modeAttention slips over long context (rare at this size; caught by the eval set)The right passage is never retrieved, so the model answers from the wrong program or guesses
Cross-referencesSeen naturally (PPE rules point to Fall Protection, LOTO points to JHA)Only if both chunks happen to be retrieved
CitationsNative, page-levelNative on retrieved chunks; page mapping must survive chunking
Cost per question (Opus 5, warm cache)About $0.13About $0.09
Time to first word3 to 8 seconds (cache read plus thinking)2 to 6 seconds (search plus thinking)
Components to build and maintainIngestion, prompt, cacheIngestion, chunking, embeddings, vector index, keyword index, reranker, retrieval evals, prompt
Updating the manualReplace one document, re-run evalsRe-chunk, re-embed, re-index, re-run retrieval and answer evals
Scales toA few manuals (roughly 500K tokens at acceptable cost)Any corpus size

Recommendation: start with the whole manual in context with prompt caching. It removes the largest source of wrong answers, it is the simplest system to build and audit, and at Opus 5 prices it costs only a few cents more per question than retrieval. Anthropic's own guidance for knowledge bases under about 200K tokens is the same: put the whole thing in the prompt and cache it.

Send the manual as plain text pages, not as the PDF file. Anthropic's PDF input renders each page as an image as well as text, at roughly 1,500 to 3,000 tokens per page. This manual has no diagrams, so that would triple the cost for nothing.

Move to hybrid retrieval when the corpus grows past roughly 500K tokens (for example, adding the NEII handbook, OSHA standards and manufacturer manuals), or when per-question cost starts to matter more than retrieval risk. The database and eval harness proposed here are ready for that step.
